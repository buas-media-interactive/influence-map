Week 1 / Influence map
01 / Understanding people

Who shapes their choices?

An influence map shows the people and places that shape how your audience finds, judges and chooses a brand. You start from them, not from the client.

Not a stakeholder map. A stakeholder map puts the client in the centre and asks who matters to the organisation. Here the audience is in the centre and you ask who matters to them.

01 / The map

One person. Four rings.

Put one persona in the middle. Place everyone and everything that influences them around it, from closest to furthest.

  • 1
    Close circle.Friends, partner, family, housemates. They shape what feels normal.
  • 2
    Gatekeepers.People and platforms at the moment of choice. They shape what is on offer.
  • 3
    Cultural voices.Creators, media, communities, other brands. They shape what is worth noticing.
  • 4
    Context and norms.Occasions, rituals, unwritten rules. They shape what feels appropriate.

04 / Watch out

Four ways it goes wrong.

Naming people by who they are.

Describe actors by what they do (“the friend who picks the bar”), not by nationality, religion or appearance. Never infer a layer. Use what people tell you.

Only positive influence.

Some actors pull people away from the brand, or from the whole category. Mark them. They often point to the real barrier.

Guesses dressed as findings.

Label every actor as hypothesis, interview or observation. Then Week 2 can change the map, and that is a good outcome.

A map nobody uses.

Actors with a strong tie should show up later as a touchpoint, a tone or a message. If none do, look at the map again.

06 / Behind the map

Three ideas to read.

The map is not one official model. It borrows from these.

Two-step flow.

Media often reach people through someone in their own circle, who passes it on and interprets it. That is your rings 1 and 3.

Katz, E., & Lazarsfeld, P. F. (1955). Personal influence: The part played by people in the flow of mass communications. Free Press.

Reference groups.

The groups people compare themselves to shape what they buy and which brands they choose, in public and in private.

Bearden, W. O., & Etzel, M. J. (1982). Reference group influence on product and brand purchase decisions. Journal of Consumer Research, 9(2), 183–194.

Weak ties.

People you know less well often bring news and options your close circle doesn’t. That is why weak ties are worth marking.

Granovetter, M. S. (1973). The strength of weak ties. American Journal of Sociology, 78(6), 1360–1380.
